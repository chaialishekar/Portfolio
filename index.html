How I work

Start with the real question. End with something people use.

I'm an Engineering Management graduate from NC State with a background in information science. Most of my work sits between the people who have a business problem and the people who build the fix. I make sure both sides mean the same thing.

1. Get the problem right

Talk to the people involved, ask what decision they actually need to make, and write it down as clear requirements before anything gets built.

2. Let the data decide

Use SQL, Python, and Power BI to test the guess against the evidence: funnels, root causes, and a no-change option to compare against.

3. Make it usable

Turn the findings into a recommendation with a dollar value, a dashboard, a test plan, or an SOP, so the team knows what to do next.

The ROI Compass: should this factory invest in AI?

A step-by-step business case that shows leaders whether AI is worth the money, before they spend it.

Why I chose this

Every company is being told to “use AI,” but most AI business cases are written before any real data exists. I wanted to show how to make that call honestly: price the guesses, compare against doing nothing, and decide anyway.

Problem
An example factory loses $3.7M a year to machine breakdowns. AI vendors promise savings, but nobody has priced the false alarms and missed failures.
What I did
Scored 12 AI use cases on 7 criteria, tuned a predictive-maintenance model for dollars instead of accuracy, compared it to a no-AI option, and tested 2 Amazon Bedrock assistants against reference answers.
Result
Catches 86% of failures, about $658K/yr net savings and a 4.7-month payback (illustrative costs), plus a gated pilot-to-production plan.

Tools: Python, Databricks, AWS S3, Amazon Bedrock, Streamlit

FeaturedData AnalyticsBusiness AnalysisIndependent project

Why the Google Merchandise Store lost half its conversions

Traffic stayed flat but sales fell. I found the one step where shoppers were dropping off and priced the fix.

Why I chose this

“Why is revenue down?” is the most common question an analyst gets, and the easiest to answer with a guess. I picked a case where traffic stayed flat but sales fell, so the only way to the answer was to follow the customer journey step by step.

Problem
Conversion fell from 1.73% to 0.89% in six weeks while sessions held steady. Was it real, and where was it happening?
What I did
Traced 267K+ GA4 sessions in SQL on BigQuery from visit to purchase, then confirmed the drop with a t-test (p = 0.00006).
Result
Pinned the loss on 67.6% cart abandonment, found returning shoppers convert 5.3× better, and built a five-part recovery plan worth about $305K a year.

Tools: SQL, BigQuery, Python, Tableau

FeaturedData AnalyticsAI / MLIndependent project

Predicting which subscribers will cancel, weeks before they do

Showed that how people watch predicts churn far better than who they are, and turned it into a daily risk list.

Why I chose this

Most churn projects stop at model accuracy. I wanted to start where a business would, with a requirements document, and end with a list a retention team could actually act on.

Problem
A streaming business only learns a subscriber is unhappy after they cancel, when it's too late to win them back.
What I did
Started from a business requirements document, then scored 5,000 subscribers with Logistic Regression and Random Forest models and grouped them into risk tiers.
Result
Three engagement signals explain about 86% of churn; the model reaches 0.98 AUC and flags 500 high-risk subscribers for retention offers.

Tools: Python, scikit-learn, BRD
